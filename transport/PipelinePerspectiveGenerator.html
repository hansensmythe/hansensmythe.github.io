Heat produced directly from manufacturing, refining, and burning the oil

In what unit would you like results displayed? They're all incredibly hot:



Each barrel of crude oil, when burned, releases megajoules (MJ) of heat. However, governments try to avoid reporting on the heat from burning, and focus on the impact of upstream production and/or refining. Upstream production involves mining, drilling, diluting into bitumen, and transportation via pipeline. The efficiency of this process—its “carbon intensity”— is typically measured in grams of CO2 equivalent per megajoule (gCO2e/MJ), though sometimes it is also reported as kilograms per barrel (kgCO2e/bbl). (1 gCO2e/MJ ≈ 6.119 kgCO2e/bbl.) Carbon intensity never includes the effect of actually burning the resulting fuel, which is why governments try to convince themselves that the environmental cost of producing crude oil may not be that bad. (The Alberta oilsands are far and away the most carbon-intensive sources of oil in North America.2)

Heat produced indirectly from producing, refining, and burning the oil

Radiative forcing due to greenhouse gases is responsible for orders of magnitude more global warming than the heat from burning the fuel. Specifically, the time-integrated radiative forcing from released CO2 exceeds the amount of heat generated by burning the oil after about 45 days for oil (~34 days for coal, and ~59 days for gas). That is, it takes only 45 days or so for the CO2 released by producing, refining, and burning the oil to capture the equivalent amount of infrared radiation emitted from sunlit Earth as was originally released from burning. Alberta's plans for growth

But what of the future? The planned Southen Route pipeline between Alberta and British Columbia is expected to carry an additional 1 million barrels of oil per day. Furthermore, the Alberta government has announced its intent by 2035 to increase production to 8 million barrels of oil per day3.

Alberta along with the Canadian federal goverment have announced a plan to reduce the carbon dioxide emitted during production4. The Pathways Project aims to keep 16 million tons of CO2 out of the atmosphere every year, reducing the emissions from the production process. However, this would only handle about 1% of the total emissions from producing, refining, and burning 8 million barrels of oil - it's a bandaid on a gaping wound, and uses unproven technology in any case. Carbon sequestration projects have a habit of over-promising and under-performing, especially when asked to scale up to meaningful results.

Model tuning controls

Since CO2 in the atmosphere causes global heating (from sunlight getting absorbed by the Earth, then radiated as heat, which then fails to get radiated back out to space because the greenhouse gases capture it), tracking how CO2 is reduced is effectively the same as tracking how much heat is added by the CO2 that remains. I’ve read that a Pulse Response Model for CO2 sequestration includes accounting for three carbon sinks which work at different rates:

  • Over years to decades, plants and the upper ocean absorb some CO2, but as the ocean absorbs CO2 it becomes more acidic, which is a whole other problem, and plants can only use CO2 if they also have good growing conditions - enough sunlight and water - and in the end will release the CO2 back into the atmosphere, unless they get buried somehow;
  • over decades to centuries, the deep ocean absorbs some CO2, but as the ocean warms and deep ocean turnover slows, this will become less efficient over time; and
  • over millennia, geological processes react with CO2 to form minerals. However, a 2023 paper in Nature10 reports that rock organic carbon oxidation CO2 release offsets the silicate weathering sink, rivalling or even exceeding the CO2 drawdown by silicate weathering at the global scale. Therefore, the geological component of the Pulse Response Model may be functionally zero.
I’ve also read that the Bern Simple Climate Model11 used by the IPCC suggests reasonable values as follows:
  • an 18.5-year half life for 34% of emissions to be absorbed by plants and the upper ocean,
  • a 173-year half life for 26% of emissions to be absorbed by the deep ocean, and
  • a 10000- to 100000-year half life for the remainder to be sequestered by geological forces.
I’ve read criticism of the Bern Model, but typically for being too conservative: it’s quite possible that global heating will exceed what I’m showing here.

I freely admit that I am not using a comprehensive climate model. I’m just providing a rough idea of how much heating is to be expected. The carbon intensity of a fuel is a measure of the greenhouse gas emissions from the extraction, refining, distribution, and use of the fuel. To help reduce emissions from oil and gas, the Clean Fuel Regulations aim to make gasoline and diesel progressively cleaner over time. The baseline carbon intensity of Canada’s gasoline is 95 gCO2e/MJ and the baseline carbon intensity of diesel is 93 gCO2e/MJ, based on 2016 levels.12 According to 5 the actual range of carbon intensity from Alberta’s oil sands ranges from 92.5 to 126.5 gCO2e/MJ, which is considerably higher than Canada’s aspirational goal by 2030 of 81 gCO2e/MJ for gasoline and 79 for diesel.
