In 2024 Alberta's total GHG emissions were 260.1 MtCO2e (260,100,000 tonnes). Oil sands (mining, in situ, upgrading) was 32.73% of that, oil and gas transmission and refining was 4.19%, and conventional oil production was 7.08%2, so (leaving out natural gas production and processing at 15.05%) 44% of Alberta's GHG emissions in 2024 were from oil (114,444,000 tons of CO2).

Adding up the barrels reported above for 2024 I get 3.98 million barrels per day, or 1453.695 million per year. We can then deduce that one tonne of CO2 is released in the generation of 12.7022 barrels of oil (1,453,695,000 barrels / 114,444,000 tons of CO2). This is an impressive figure, if accurate: I've seen estimates that are more like four to five times higher. But let's use the official Alberta government figures.

We can also determine how much CO2 is released per barrel burned. Envorem Calculations And Sources Used Across Our Site says that 2.79 tonnes of CO2 are generated per tonne of oil burned. And What Is a Barrel of Oil Weight and Why Does It Matter in Industrial Applications? says that a barrel of crude oil masses between 136 and 143 kilograms. Therefore, I deduce that between 379.44 and 399 kg of CO2 is released per barrel burned.

Now we have a ratio between produced CO2 and burned CO2. I think it's reasonable to infer that the same ratio would apply to the produced heat and the burned heat.

Alberta current produces over four million barrels of crude oil per day, or about 1.4 billion barrels per year. Each barrel of crude oil, when burned, releases megajoules (MJ) of heat. However, governments try to avoid reporting on the heat from burning, and focus on the impact of upstream production and/or refining.

Upstream production involves mining, drilling, diluting into bitumen, and transportation via pipeline, rail, and tanker. Refining turns the crude oil into gasoline, jet fuel, diesel, fuel oils, petrochemical feedstocks, asphalt, etc.

Heat produced indirectly from producing, refining, and burning the oil

Unfortunately, it turns out that the heat from burning the fuel is several orders of magnitude smaller than the radiative forcing due to greenhouse gases. Specifically, the radiative forcing from released CO2 exceeds the amount of heat generated by burning the oil after about 45 days for oil (~34 days for coal, and ~59 days for gas) 3. That is, it takes only 45 days or so for the CO2 released by producing, refining, and burning the oil to capture the equivalent amount of infrared radiation emitted from sunlit Earth as was originally released from burning.

I don’t think “carbon footprint” is an appropriate term for what we’re discussing here. You use “carbon footprint” to estimate your current consumption of resources, but the CO2 is your “carbon legacy”.

Alberta’s plans for growth

But what of the future? The planned Southen Route pipeline between Alberta and British Columbia is expected to carry an additional 1 million barrels of oil per day. Furthermore, the Alberta government has announced its intent by 2035 to increase production to 8 million barrels of oil per day 4 despite the fact that the Alberta oil sands are among the most carbon-intensive sources of crude oil in North America5.

Alberta, along with the Canadian federal goverment, has announced a plan to reduce the carbon dioxide emitted during production. The Pathways Project6 aims to keep 16 million tons of CO2 out of the atmosphere every year, reducing the emissions from the production process. However, this would only handle about 1% of the total emissions from producing, refining, and burning 8 million barrels of oil - it’s a bandaid on a gaping wound, and uses unproven technology in any case. Carbon sequestration projects have a habit of over-promising and under-performing, especially when asked to scale up to meaningful results.7 I am therefore not bothering to include the minuscule effect that Pathways might eventually provide.

Model tuning controls

Since CO2 in the atmosphere causes global heating (from sunlight getting absorbed by the Earth, then radiated as heat, which then fails to get radiated back out to space because the greenhouse gases capture it), tracking how CO2 is reduced is effectively the same as tracking how much heat is added by the CO2 that remains. I’ve read that a Pulse Response Model for CO2 sequestration includes accounting for three carbon sinks which work at different rates:

  • Over years to decades, plants and the upper ocean absorb some CO2, but as the ocean absorbs CO2 it becomes more acidic, which is a whole other problem, and plants can only use CO2 if they also have good growing conditions - enough sunlight and water - and in the end will release the CO2 back into the atmosphere, unless they get buried somehow;
  • over decades to centuries, the deep ocean absorbs some CO2, but as the ocean warms and deep ocean turnover slows, this will become less efficient over time; and
  • over millennia, geological processes react with CO2 to form minerals. However, a 2023 paper in Nature8 reports that rock organic carbon oxidation CO2 release offsets the silicate weathering sink, rivalling or even exceeding the CO2 drawdown by silicate weathering at the global scale. Therefore, the geological component of the Pulse Response Model may be functionally zero.
I’ve also read that the Bern Simple Climate Model9 used by the IPCC suggests reasonable values as follows:
  • an 18.5-year half life for 34% of emissions to be absorbed by plants and the upper ocean,
  • a 173-year half life for 26% of emissions to be absorbed by the deep ocean, and
  • a 10000- to 100000-year half life for the remainder to be sequestered by geological forces.
I’ve read criticism of the Bern Model, but typically for being too conservative: it’s quite possible that global heating will exceed what I’m showing here.

I freely admit that I am not using a comprehensive climate model. I’m just providing a rough idea of how much heating is to be expected. These graphs calculate the running total amount of heat that will get added to the atmosphere by the CO2 that remains after these three processes have sequestered some of it.
