Let's say you want to go on holiday. Although you're probably aware that the latest data from CERES for May, 2026 shows that the Earth is warming at a rate of about 14 Hiroshima nuclear bombs (~63 terajoules, or 63,000,000 MJ) per second, or about 1,200,000 per day due to an excess of atmospheric greenhouse gases, you might assume that doesn't apply to you because, after all, it's just one flight.

When sunlight hits the Earth, the energy is radiated back out to space as longwave infrared radiation (heat). However, greenhouse gases such as CO2 trap this radiation, causing the atmosphere to warm until the greenhouse gases are no longer present.

This page will help you figure out what your personal contribution is to that total. For example, if you live in Kelowna BC and you want to holiday in Mexico, you'll take a short 1-hour hop in a Boeing 737-700 to Calgary, then transfer to a Boeing 737 MAX 8 for the 5.5-hour 4000 km flight. In the process, they'll warm the earth directly by burning the fuel, generating over 900,000 MJ of heat: about 1.4% of a Hiroshima.

Notwithstanding the direct heating, if we assume that the fuel was distilled from Alberta's oil sands*, the first flight will be responsible for releasing about 14 tonnes of CO2 (from mining, drilling, transportation, refining, and burning), and the second about 85 tonnes, for about 100 tonnes total. What happens to that CO2? Effectively: nothing. Of the three ways that greenhouse gases are removed from the atmosphere (plant and ocean absorption, deep ocean, and rock weathering), the last two take centuries to millenia to be effective, and the first one doesn't work because we have overwhelmed plant and ocean carbon sinks, as is evident from the contining rise in atmospheric CO2 concentrations since we started measuring such things.

Therefore, these flights spawn what I call "ghost flights" that keep flying, effectively in perpetuity. How often does each ghost flight fly? According to Time scales and ratios of climate forcing due to thermal versus carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels (Geophysical Research Letters / Volume 42, Issue 11), within two months. In the short term, we can then calculate how many years it will take before they warm the Earth by one Hiroshima: 63,000,000 / 900,000, then divide by 6 to convert years to 2-month periods: 11.6 years.

But wait, this is a holiday, therefore these are return tickets, so it's actually more like 5.8 years.

In August 1945 when U.S. president Harry Truman had to decide whether to use an atomic bomb this powerful, I'm sure he wrestled with his conscience. I suspect the only reason why people typically don't have second thoughts about this is that when you fly, you don't get an enormous flash and a big mushroom cloud all at once. It just builds up over a few years. If someone had been exploding atomic bombs off the coast of Europe in the summer of 2026, people wouldn't be wondering why it was getting so effing hot, but we're doing the equivalent by continuing to abuse fossil fuels for trivial pursuits such as driving and flying for pleasure.
