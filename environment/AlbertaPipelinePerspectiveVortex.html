There’ll Be a Hot Time in the Old Future Tonight

A few years ago I heard perhaps the most perspicacious question ever asked on CBC’s radio science show, Quirks and Quarks: How much of global warming could be attributed to direct human caused heating from burning fossil fuels versus “indirect” heating from the greenhouse effect from the resulting CO2 and sunlight? It was answered by Greg Flato, Senior Research Scientist with Environment and Climate Change Canada in Victoria, who said:

The carbon dioxide that we put into the atmosphere lingers in the atmosphere for a very long time. Carbon dioxide has a lifetime in the atmosphere that is thousands of years long, whereas the flame that comes from burning that lump of coal only lasts for a short time, until the lump of coal is burned up. After about 30 to 60 days, the amount of indirect heating due to the greenhouse gases equals the direct heating that came from the combustion.

Then in 2026 when Prime Minister Carney and Premier Danielle Smith announced plans for a new pipeline to transport a million barrels per day of crude oil from the Alberta oil sands to a deep-water Pacific port in British Columbia, I wondered how much actual global warming would be produced, not just from mining, drilling, diluting into bitumen, and transporting via pipeline, rail, and tanker, but from refining and eventually burning the resulting products. It occurred to me that if I knew how much energy was released by burning a barrel of crude oil, and how much CO2 was generated by that burning, and how much CO2 was released in production and refining, I could actually calculate the heating effects of the new pipeline, and by extension, existing pipelines and other future projects.

Where did Dr. Flato get his figure of 30 to 60 days to equal the warming? That depends on the fuel. According to Time scales and ratios of climate forcing due to thermal versus carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels by Xiaochun Zhang and Ken Caldeira, the radiative forcing from released CO2 exceeds the amount of heat generated by burning oil after about 45 days, and about 34 days for coal, and about 59 days for methane (natural gas).

And where did he get the lifetime of CO2 in the atmosphere? The Bern Simple Climate Model (BernSCM) v1.0 published by the European Geosciences Union Geoscientific Model Development (Articles, Volume 11, Issue 5, 25 May 2018) describes types of carbon sequestration that operate at different time scales:

  • plants and upper ocean (years to decades),
  • deep ocean (centuries), and
  • geological processes (millennia)

Unfortunately, all three are two-way processes—not so much “sinks” as “banks”. For example, when a tree grows it absorbs CO2, but when it dies it releases CO2. The upper ocean absorbs CO2 (and becomes more acidic, but that’s a different problem), but depending upon the partial pressure, will release that CO2. The deep ocean only absorbs CO2 if and when the thermohaline circulation is effective, such as that driving the AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation), and that’s becoming less effective as surface water becomes less salty due to melting ice caps. Even the rocks may be two-way streets for CO2: a recent paper in Nature reports that rock organic carbon oxidation CO2 release offsets the silicate weathering sink, rivalling or even exceeding the CO2 drawdown by silicate weathering at the global scale. Therefore, the geological component of the Pulse Response Model may be functionally zero, at least on human timescales.

Therefore, the naïve notion that CO2 is quickly and permanently absorbed by growing plants does not accurately or adequately explain what we’re seeing, which is a continuous and accelerating buildup of CO2 ever since we first began to measure it. Some of what we put into the air now is still going to be there in thousands of years, and is going to be duplicating the heat from combustion every 45 days. Nevertheless, some CO2 is indeed absorbed, so we may be able to use a Pulse Response Model to estimate the annual reduction of CO2 to infer a matching reduction in radiative forcing.

Let’s see where we’re at, for Alberta oil at least, and go from there.

Firstly, how much heat is generated from burning one barrel of crude oil? According to Statistics Canada, burning 1 barrel of crude oil = 6.193 gigajoules, or 0.6193 terajoules (TJ) of heat. For comparison’s sake, the Little Boy atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima released 62.8 to 66.9 ± 8.4 TJ—about 108 times more energy than is in a single barrel of oil.

In 2024 Alberta’s total GHG emissions were 260.1 MtCO2e (260,100,000 tonnes). Oil sands (mining, in situ, upgrading) was 32.73% of that, oil and gas transmission and refining was 4.19%, and conventional oil production was 7.08%10, so (leaving out natural gas production and processing at 15.05%) 44% of Alberta’s GHG emissions in 2024 were from oil (114,444,000 tons of CO2).

Adding up the barrels reported above for 2024 I get 3.98 million barrels per day, or 1453.695 million per year. We can then deduce that one tonne of CO2 is released in the generation of 12.7022 barrels of oil (1,453,695,000 barrels / 114,444,000 tons of CO2). This is an impressive figure, if accurate: I’ve seen estimates that are several times higher. But let’s use the official Alberta government figures.

We can also determine how much CO2 is released per barrel burned. Envorem Calculations And Sources Used Across Our Site says that 2.79 mass units of CO2 are generated per mass unit of oil burned, although I’ve seen higher figures such as 3.15. And What Is a Barrel of Oil Weight and Why Does It Matter in Industrial Applications? says that a barrel of crude oil masses between 136 and 143 kilograms. Therefore, I deduce that between 379.44 and 399 kg of CO2 is released per barrel burned (or using the 3.15 ratio, 428.4 to 450.45 kg of CO2). The extra mass in the CO2 comes from the oxygen in the air.

Now we have a ratio between CO2 released from crude oil production and CO2 released from crude oil combustion. I think it’s reasonable to infer that the same ratio would apply between the produced heat and the burned heat.

The Carbon Legacy: heat produced indirectly from producing, refining, and burning the oil

I don’t think “carbon footprint” is an appropriate term for what we’re discussing here. You use “carbon footprint” to estimate your current consumption of resources, but the CO2 left in the atmosphere is your “carbon legacy”—what you leave for posterity.

Alberta’s plans for growth

But what of the future? The planned Southen Route pipeline between Alberta and British Columbia is expected to carry an additional 1 million barrels of oil per day. Furthermore, the Alberta government has announced its intent by 2035 to increase production to 8 million barrels of oil per day 11 despite the fact that the Alberta oil sands are among the most carbon-intensive sources of crude oil in North America12.

Alberta, along with the Canadian federal goverment, has announced a plan to reduce the carbon dioxide emitted during production. The Pathways Project13 aims to keep 16 million tons of CO2 out of the atmosphere every year, reducing the emissions from the production process. However, this would only handle about 1% of the total emissions from producing, refining, and burning 8 million barrels of oil - it’s a bandaid on a gaping wound, and uses unproven technology in any case. Carbon sequestration projects have a habit of over-promising and under-performing, especially when asked to scale up to meaningful results.14 I am therefore not bothering to include the minuscule effect that Pathways might eventually provide.

Conclusion

I grew up knowing about the relationship between greenhouse gas emissions and global warming, but only in terms of global average temperature rise, or millions of tons of CO2, neither of which really stir the imagination or help us to understand how much energy we are pumping into the ecosphere. I hope that the truly horrifying numbers that I can show, resulting only from a couple of dozen years of oil production, may prompt readers to reconsider their priorities. Should I try to calculate my personal carbon legacy for my tombstone? No—there’s no point. My CO2 will still be there, acidifying the ocean, exterminating species, causing weather disasters, and pushing climate change, long after my tombstone erodes down to illegibility. Our graves need no flowers: the air will remember our names.

Model tuning controls

Since CO2 in the atmosphere causes global heating (from sunlight getting absorbed by the Earth, then radiated as heat, which then fails to get radiated back out to space because the greenhouse gases capture it), tracking how CO2 is reduced is effectively the same as tracking how much heat is added by the CO2 that remains. I’ve read that a Pulse Response Model for CO2 sequestration includes accounting for three carbon sinks which work at different rates. I’ve also read that the Bern Simple Climate Model4 used by the IPCC suggests reasonable values as follows:

  • an 18.5-year half life for 34% of emissions to be absorbed by plants and the upper ocean,
  • a 173-year half life for 26% of emissions to be absorbed by the deep ocean, and
  • a 10000- to 100000-year half life for the remainder to be sequestered by geological forces.
I’ve read criticism of the Bern Model, but typically for being too conservative: it’s quite possible that global heating will exceed what I’m showing here.

I freely admit that I am not using a comprehensive climate model. I’m just providing a rough idea of how much heating is to be expected.
