There’ll Be a Hot Time in the Old Future Tonight

Abstract

 Global warming is typically reported in terms of rising global average temperature, or number of tons of CO2 added to the atmosphere. The problem is that neither of these metrics helps the lay person understand exactly how much heat is getting added, how quickly, and for how long.

Using data about oil produced by the province of Alberta, Canada, this article calculates the actual heat of global warming in units that may be easier to comprehend. The global warming caused by greenhouse gas emissions from oil production, refining, and combustion is laid out, both annually and cumulatively, over periods ranging from centuries to millennia.

The following charts are shown:

  • Alberta oil production by type from 2012 to 2026 (in 2026, a little over 4 million barrels per day).
  • Immediate heating just from the heat of production, refining, and combustion of the oil.
  • The “carbon legacy” of this production: the global heating, year by year, resulting from that oil.
  • The effect of Alberta’s plans for growth of its oil sector: what are the global heating implications?
  • The very long term (millennia) cumulative heat impact of the use of this amount of oil.

Controls are provided for users to adjust settings to explore various scenarios. For example, what happens if production continues to rise and gradually tapers off, versus a catastrophic shutdown? and how far into the future might significant heating continue?

Controls are also provided for users to adjust the underlying model of how carbon is slowly drawn out of the atmosphere by plants, the ocean, and the rocks.

The conclusion is that global heating is both significant and long-term, and a proper understanding of exactly how much heat is being produced over what time period is essential for both government and the laity.

Key points

  • Crude oil production, refining, and combustion all produce heat and generate carbon dioxide (CO2), a powerful greenhouse gas.

    • How much heat is generated from burning one barrel of crude oil? According to Statistics Canada, burning 1 barrel of crude oil = 6.193 gigajoules, or 0.6193 terajoules (TJ) of heat. For comparison’s sake, the Little Boy atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima released 62.8 to 66.9 ± 8.4 TJ—about 108 times more energy than is in a single barrel of oil.

    In 2024 Alberta’s total GHG emissions were 260.1 MtCO2e (260,100,000 tonnes). Oil sands (mining, in situ, upgrading) was 32.73% of that, oil and gas transmission and refining was 4.19%, and conventional oil production was 7.08%3, so (leaving out natural gas production and processing at 15.05%) 44% of Alberta’s GHG emissions in 2024 were from oil (114,444,000 tons of CO2).

    In 2024 Alberta produced an average of 3.98 million barrels per day, or 1453.695 million per year. We can therefore deduce that for Alberta crude oil production, one tonne of CO2 is released in the generation of 12.7022 barrels of oil (1,453,695,000 barrels / 114,444,000 tons of CO2). This is an impressive figure, if accurate: I’ve seen estimates that are several times higher. But let’s use the official Alberta government figures.

  • The radiative forcing caused by greenhouse gases released from oil production, refining, and combustion equals the heat that came from these activities very quickly: after only about 45 days.

    • According to Time scales and ratios of climate forcing due to thermal versus carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels by Xiaochun Zhang and Ken Caldeira, the radiative forcing from released CO2 exceeds the amount of heat generated by burning oil after about 45 days, and about 34 days for coal, and about 59 days for methane (natural gas).

  • Only a small amount of those greenhouse gases are quickly absorbed by plants or the ocean; the rest stays in the atmosphere for a long time.

    • The Bern Simple Climate Model (BernSCM) v1.0 published by the European Geosciences Union Geoscientific Model Development (Articles, Volume 11, Issue 5, 25 May 2018) describes types of carbon sequestration that operate at different time scales:

    • plants and upper ocean (years to decades),
    • deep ocean (centuries), and
    • geological processes (millennia)

    Unfortunately, all three are two-way processes—not so much “sinks” as “banks”. For example, when a tree grows it absorbs CO2, but when it dies it releases CO2. The upper ocean absorbs CO2 (and becomes more acidic, but that’s a different problem), but depending upon the partial pressure, will release that CO2. The deep ocean only absorbs CO2 if and when the thermohaline circulation is effective, such as that driving the AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation), and that’s becoming less effective as surface water becomes less salty due to melting ice caps. Even the rocks may be two-way streets for CO2: a recent paper in Nature reports that rock organic carbon oxidation CO2 release offsets the silicate weathering sink, rivalling or even exceeding the CO2 drawdown by silicate weathering at the global scale. Therefore, the geological component of the Pulse Response Model may be functionally zero, at least on human timescales.

    Therefore, the naïve notion that CO2 is quickly and permanently absorbed by growing plants does not accurately or adequately explain what we’re seeing, which is a continuous and accelerating buildup of CO2 ever since we first began to measure it. Some of what we put into the air now is still going to be there in thousands of years, and is going to be duplicating the heat from combustion every 45 days. Nevertheless, some CO2 is indeed absorbed, so once we estimate the annual reduction of CO2, we can derive a matching reduction in radiative forcing.

  • Since we know how much heat is generated by burning a barrel of crude oil, if we also know how much CO2 is released in the process, we can model how much CO2 is absorbed over time, and how much remains to continue to trap infrared radiation and heat the planet.

    • How much CO2 is released per barrel burned? Envorem Calculations And Sources Used Across Our Site says that 2.79 mass units of CO2 are generated per mass unit of oil burned, although I’ve seen higher figures such as 3.15. And What Is a Barrel of Oil Weight and Why Does It Matter in Industrial Applications? says that a barrel of crude oil masses between 136 and 143 kilograms. Therefore, I deduce that between 379.44 and 399 kg of CO2 is released per barrel burned (or using the 3.15 ratio, 428.4 to 450.45 kg of CO2). The extra mass in the CO2 comes from the oxygen in the air.

    Having a ratio between CO2 released from crude oil production and CO2 released from crude oil combustion allows us to infer the ratio between the produced heat and the burned heat.

  • Therefore, we can calculate the actual amount of global heating due to radiative forcing caused by greenhouse gases emitted from a specific number of barrels of crude oil over a long time period. This is its “carbon legacy”.

    • I don’t think “carbon footprint” is an appropriate term for what we’re discussing here. You use “carbon footprint” to estimate your current consumption of resources, but the CO2 left in the atmosphere is your “carbon legacy”—what you leave for posterity.

    Alberta current produces over four million barrels of crude oil per day, or almost 1.5 billion barrels per year. To a first approximation, multiply the heat generated by burning oil by 8 to get the amount of heat generated annually from the CO2 released by burning. This ratio goes down as CO2 is absorbed, but this is not a quick process.

  • Applying this calculation to current and planned oil production from Alberta's oil sands reveals interesting results.

    • In 2026 Prime Minister Carney and Premier Danielle Smith announced plans for a new pipeline to transport a million barrels per day of crude oil from the Alberta oil sands to a deep-water Pacific port in British Columbia. Furthermore, the Alberta government has announced its intent by 2035 to increase production to 8 million barrels of oil per day 7 despite the fact that the Alberta oil sands are among the most carbon-intensive sources of crude oil in North America8.

    However, Alberta, along with the Canadian federal goverment, has announced a plan to reduce the carbon dioxide emitted during production. The Pathways Project9 aims to keep 16 million tons of CO2 out of the atmosphere every year, reducing the emissions from the production process. Unfortunately, this would only handle about 1% of the total emissions from producing, refining, and burning 8 million barrels of oil - it’s a bandaid on a gaping wound, and uses unproven technology in any case. Carbon sequestration projects have a habit of over-promising and under-performing, especially when required to scale up to meaningful results.10

Model tuning controls

Since CO2 in the atmosphere causes global heating (from sunlight getting absorbed by the Earth, then radiated as heat, which then fails to get radiated back out to space because the greenhouse gases capture it), tracking how CO2 is reduced is effectively the same as tracking how much heat is added by the CO2 that remains. I’ve read that a Pulse Response Model for CO2 sequestration includes accounting for three carbon sinks which work at different rates. I’ve also read that the Bern Simple Climate Model5 used by the IPCC suggests reasonable values as follows:

  • an 18.5-year half life for 34% of emissions to be absorbed by plants and the upper ocean,
  • a 173-year half life for 26% of emissions to be absorbed by the deep ocean, and
  • a 10000- to 100000-year half life for the remainder to be sequestered by geological forces.
I’ve read criticism of the Bern Model, but typically for being too conservative: it’s quite possible that global heating will exceed what I’m showing here.

I freely admit that I am not using a comprehensive climate model. I’m just providing a rough idea of how much heating is to be expected. These graphs calculate the running total amount of heat that will get added to the atmosphere by the CO2 that remains after these three processes have sequestered some of it. Conclusion

I grew up knowing about the relationship between greenhouse gas emissions and global warming, but only in terms of global average temperature rise, or millions of tons of CO2, neither of which really stir the imagination or help us to understand how much energy we are pumping into the ecosphere. I hope that the truly horrifying numbers that I can show, resulting only from a couple of dozen years of oil production, may prompt readers to reconsider their priorities. Should I try to calculate my personal carbon legacy for my tombstone? No—there’s no point. My CO2 will still be there, acidifying the ocean, exterminating species, causing weather disasters, and pushing climate change, long after my tombstone erodes down to illegibility.

Our graves need no flowers: the air has recorded our names.13
